A data story about the online grocery aisle · draft, November 2026

Sold on pleasure

We matched 75,933 Amazon grocery listings to their ingredient records. The ultra-processed ones are sold with fun, cravings and convenience. The less processed ones are sold with certificates and things they leave out.

The aisle

Amazon's grocery department lists over 600,000 products. More than half carry a barcode, the same number a supermarket scanner reads. That barcode lets us look each product up in Open Food Facts, a volunteer database of ingredient lists.

Open Food Facts sorts products into four NOVA groups. Group 1 is food as it grows: rice, beans, apples. Group 4 is ultra-processed: products assembled from industrial ingredients like emulsifiers, flavourings and modified starch. Seven in ten of the products we could match are in group 4.

For every matched product we have two kinds of text. The seller's marketing copy (bullet points and description), and the shoppers' reviews.

Two vocabularies

If you compare all ultra-processed copy with all other copy, you mostly learn that candy is ultra-processed. So we compared like with like. In each of 25 food categories we took the same number of ultra-processed and less processed products, 10,349 of each in total, and asked which words each side uses more.

The ultra-processed side talks about pleasure and ease: flavored, fun, irresistible, satisfy, favorite, creamy, cheesy, and about portions and speed: individually, packet, quick, minutes, school. The less processed side talks about purity and paperwork: non-GMO, certified, USDA, verified, harvested, slowly, batches, and often about what is not in the product: no additives, no chemicals, no pesticides.

A second measure agrees. We scored every word for how pleasant it feels, from −1 to +1, using a crowd-rated word list (the NRC Valence lexicon). Ultra-processed copy averages 0.286, other copy 0.269. Within the same category the gap is +0.014. It is small, and about half of it comes from food words with sunny scores, like candy and dessert. The rest is the split above: pleasure words on one side, health words on the other.

Do shoppers feel it?

The marketing is a little sunnier for ultra-processed food. The reviews are not. Measured the same way, reviews of both kinds average almost exactly the same pleasantness: 0.207 against 0.207.

What changes is how people praise. Reviews of ultra-processed products say love, favorite, yummy, kids. Reviews of less processed products say great, excellent, healthy, organic. Shoppers echo the vocabulary they were sold.

The "treat" trap

Our first emotion count said reviews of ultra-processed food carry more anger, disgust and fear. Then we read the reviews. The biggest single cause was the word treat, which appears 6.9 times per 10,000 words in ultra-processed reviews and 3.3 times in the rest. The emotion lexicon tags treat with anger, disgust and fear. Shoppers mean "a great morning treat".

Other culprits were broken (cookies damaged in the post) and bear (gummy bears). We dropped that finding. The genuine complaint words, like disappointed and horrible, are only slightly more common.

Who reviews what

Now picture the shoppers. Link two products whenever a surprisingly large number of the same people reviewed both. Below is the dense core of that network: 1,328 products and 9,908 links.

Ultra-processed products sit with ultra-processed products. One number captures it, assortativity: 0 means links ignore processing, 1 means links only ever join products of the same kind. The full network scores +0.28.

Some of that is expected. People who buy snacks review snacks, and snacks are mostly ultra-processed. Brands also tend to be all one kind. So we shuffled the colours in stricter ways: only among products of the same category, then only among products of the same brand. The real network beats every shuffle, but brand explains most of the pattern.

So the honest version is this. Shoppers do sort themselves by processing level, a bit beyond what category and brand alone would produce, but mostly by sticking to brands of one kind.

Beyond the grocery aisle

The same anonymous reviewer IDs appear across Amazon's other departments, so we can ask what else the two kinds of grocery shoppers review. We took 141,977 reviewers with at least three reviews of matched groceries and compared two groups: people whose grocery reviews are all ultra-processed (37,287) and people for whom ultra-processed is at most half (40,577). We compared people with similar numbers of grocery reviews, and wrote our predictions down before downloading the other departments.

The ultra-processed side of the network is also the games-and-toys side. Its reviewers are about 4 to 5 points more likely to review video games and toys, and about as much less likely to review sports gear and household health products. Baby products show no difference. These are review habits of pseudonymous accounts, not household profiles, and the gaps are modest. They do suggest that processing level is part of a wider way of shopping.

Purity travels, pleasure stays home

Does the pleasure language follow ultra-processed shoppers into other aisles? It does not. The toys, pet supplies and household goods they review are marketed just as pleasantly as the ones the other group reviews; across 1.6 million reviews in seven departments the gap is zero (every department within ±0.006 on the −1 to +1 scale).

The purity vocabulary does travel. Comparing the marketing copy of non-food products that lean towards each group, the less processed shoppers' side reads non-GMO, potency, no fillers, no preservatives, USDA, tested: chlorella, organic cinnamon capsules, vitamins. The ultra-processed shoppers' side reads durable, comfortable, portable, stains, birthday, holiday: baby wash, kids' paint books, batteries, a potty.

And they write differently. Ultra-processed shoppers' reviews in other departments are warmer (+0.011 on the same scale, higher in six of seven departments) and full of love, perfect, gift, fun, daughter, birthday. The other group writes about weight, stomach, gut, effective, formula.

Can we trust the pleasantness score?

We checked it against star ratings on 200,000 grocery reviews. Average pleasantness climbs with every star, from 0.06 at one star to 0.34 at five, and the score tells one- and two-star reviews from four- and five-star reviews apart 89% of the time (AUC 0.89; shuffled stars give 0.50). A classifier trained on the stars does better (0.99), mainly because it understands "not": our word list scores "Not happy" as happy.

The rich get richer

A grocery product with one review before 2018 gained 0.4 new reviews during 2018. One with about a thousand gained about 190. Plot one against the other on log scales and the points fall on a line with slope 0.93: attention is handed out almost exactly in proportion to the attention a product already has. That is the mechanism behind the heavy tail. Most of the 600,000 grocery listings have a handful of reviews; 0.2% have more than a thousand.

The hubs are not food

Which products hold the shopping network together? We ranked products by PageRank, the measure that rewards being linked to other well-linked products. The top of the list is everyday staples: Amazon Basics AA and AAA batteries, essential oils, tissues, trash bags, cat litter, dog dental treats, a pain cream. Of the top 100, 48 are household goods, 30 pet supplies and 21 groceries, and those grocery hubs are no more ultra-processed than groceries in general (72% against 70%).

What did not hold up

  • "Sold on joy." Counting joy words in marketing copy gave a small difference that vanished on our most reliable matches. The pleasantness score held; the joy count did not.
  • Anger, disgust and fear in reviews. Mostly the word treat. See above.
  • Trust words in marketing. No reliable difference.
  • Pleasure everywhere. We expected ultra-processed shoppers' other purchases to be marketed more pleasantly too. They are not.
  • Promise versus experience, measured with emotion categories. No difference. Measured with pleasantness, the gap between sunny copy and plain reviews is wider for ultra-processed food (+0.009), but small.

How we did it

Amazon grocery listings and reviews from Amazon Reviews 2023 (Hou et al., 2024, McAuley Lab), used for research only. Products matched by UPC barcode to Open Food Facts; NOVA groups are assigned by Open Food Facts from ingredient lists. Contains data from Open Food Facts, available under the Open Database License (ODbL). Word pleasantness from the NRC Valence, Arousal, and Dominance Lexicon v2.1 and emotions from the NRC Word-Emotion Association Lexicon (Saif M. Mohammad and Peter Turney, National Research Council Canada), used for non-commercial research. Words that are ingredient names were removed before scoring. All comparisons are within food categories and tested with label shuffles. Hypotheses were written down before the tests were run. The department comparison links the same pseudonymous reviewer IDs across Amazon Reviews 2023 category files; no profile data was collected and no one was identified.

Limits: reviewers are not all buyers; Amazon is one shop; about one match in seven is another flavour of the same brand; lexicon scores measure word associations, not feelings; all effects are small.
