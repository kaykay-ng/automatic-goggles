International and European context In 2015 the Paris Agreement was concluded under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC).
The central objective of this agreement is to limit the increase in global temperature to a level well below 2°C, and to aim for a maximum increase of 1.5°C (compared to the pre-industrial level).
To this end, the various parts of the agreement presented a determined contribution at national level by 2025/2030.
In addition, all parties are invited to develop and submit to the UNFCCC for 2020 a long-term low-emission greenhouse gas development strategy.
In preparation for the EU's long-term strategy to be presented to the UNFCCC in 2020, the European Commission published in November 2018 its long-term strategic vision "A clean planet for all", which aims to build a prosperous, modern, competitive and climate neutral economy by 2050 1.
According to the Commission, this ambition is in line with the 1.5°C objective set out in the Paris Agreement.
The Commission's vision has been accompanied by a detailed and in-depth analysis, with different scenarios exploring how climate neutrality can be achieved by 2050.
At the European Council on 12 and 13 December 2019, European Heads of Government endorsed the goal of a climate-neutral Europe by 2050, and the Commission was invited to prepare a proposal for a long-term strategy as soon as possible in 2020, with a view to its adoption by the Council and submission to the UNFCCC.
Article 15 of the Governance Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2018/1999) also requires each Member State to develop and submit a long-term strategy to the European Commission before 1 January 2020.
Through this long-term strategy, Belgium wishes to meet the expectations of the Paris Agreement and the European Governance Regulation, and to provide a clear framework for its citizens and businesses.
Belgian context and approach Belgium is a federal state in which decision-making power is shared between a federal state, three regions (walloon, Flemish and Brussels-Capital) and three Communities (Flamand, French and German-speaking).
In climate policy, regions have important responsibilities in areas such as rational use of energy, promotion of renewable energy sources, public transport, transport infrastructure, urban and rural development, agriculture and waste management.
The federal state is responsible for much of the tax policy.
It is also responsible for product policy (standards, fuel quality, labelling and performance standards for electrical appliances for domestic or industrial use, etc.).
It is responsible for the security of the country's energy supply, and for nuclear energy.
It oversees territorial waters and is therefore responsible for the development of offshore wind farms.

The federal level wishes to cooperate with and support regional entities in their transition to a climate neutral society.

Public consultations In light of the above-mentioned approach that each region has developed its own long-term strategy, the regions have also been able to decide separately on the organization of public consultations.
Several stakeholder and public consultations on climate and energy policy have already been held in recent years, both at Belgian level (e.g. in the framework of the inter-federal energy pact and the Belgian national energy-climate plan) and at regional level (in the framework of regional policy strategies and plans).
In addition, in recent years several technical studies and explorations on long-term greenhouse gas reduction scenarios have been carried out (both at Belgian and regional level).
For reasons of efficiency, each entity has chosen to base its strategy on the work of studies and the contributions of existing stakeholders, rather than organizing an additional public consultation.
Each region has also indicated in its strategy that this is not to be considered as a final point.
Each region undertakes to develop journeys for the coming months/years in order to continue the discussion on regional strategies with the public, the various experts and stakeholders involved, and to support and develop them in more detail if necessary.
The federal level also wants to cooperate and support the different levels of power, and stakeholders in the development of the identified elements.

Electricity Sector Each region recognizes that electricity generation must be climate neutral by 2050 6.
Therefore, each strategy relies heavily on renewable energy sources, including solar and wind energy, taking into account the potential of each region.
Strategies differ somewhat on the role of biomass in the electricity mix: in Walloon and Flemish strategies, the use of sustainable biomass for energy purposes still has a role to play (taking into account the cascade principle), while in the Brussels strategy, the use of biofuels is considered inappropriate in an urban context, given the impact on air quality.
Each region also anticipates that electrification - combined with a climate neutral electricity supply - will contribute significantly to the reduction of greenhouse gases in other sectors, including transportation (e.g., electric vehicles), buildings (e.g., heat pumps) and industry.
At the same time, each strategy indicates that the own potential for renewable electricity generation (and for energy generation in general) is insufficient to fully cover the expected future demand.
Each region therefore attributes an important role in its long-term strategy to the import of climate-neutral electricity (and other energy sources).
The strategies of the Flemish and Walloon regions also indicate the need for a reform of the current centralized, demand-driven energy system towards a decentralized and flexible system, as well as the need for electricity storage solutions for both short (e.g. batteries) and long periods (e.g. batteries) of time.
The federal level can support, within its competence, the transition in the energy sector, inter alia, by maximizing the potential of offshore wind and focusing on demand management, energy storage and the interconnection/integration of Belgium into the European electricity grid and the possible construction of the infrastructure necessary for the import of carbon neutral energy sources.
With these levers, the federal level helps to ensure reliable, affordable and climate-neutral energy supply, and to facilitate electrification in other sectors.
In addition, the federal level, in consultation with the regions, could support the transition by: - A long-term vision for electricity supply; - Transport infrastructure for green hydrogen and synthetic fuels; - A long-term framework for the integration of intermittent sources by all actors; - Support for an active role for citizens.

In the industrial sectors themselves, efforts will be pursued primarily to improve energy efficiency.
An important point of attention in this respect is the maximum recovery of residual heat, both within the plant and via heat networks.
In addition, the shift to renewable and climate neutral energy and raw materials will contribute to further emission reductions.
To this end, both direct electrification and other climate-neutral energy vectors, such as green hydrogen and other synthetic fuels, are envisaged.
Biofuels will also have a role to play, particularly in industrial processes where electrification or other carbon-free energy vehicles are not an option.
However, both strategies argue that the recovery of biomass as a raw material is preferable to its combustion as a fuel.

In a broader context, each strategy (including that of the Brussels-Capital Region) mentions the importance of a transition to a circular economy, in which products are designed, manufactured and consumed in an alternative way.
In addition to creating added value and reducing the consumption of raw materials, the transition to a circular economy should also contribute significantly to the reduction of greenhouse gases, both in the industrial sector and in other sectors (see also point 4.5 below).
The federal level can contribute, within its competence, in particular in the field of product standardization and taxation, to the transition to a circular economy, for example by focusing on a longer life, better repairability, better demonstrability and less material intensity of products.

4.4 Main axes of adaptation policy Belgium is already experiencing the consequences of climate change, and the impact is expected to increase with the rise of the average global temperature.
The country must therefore organize itself to adapt to the expected or already real consequences and to manage the risks associated with them.
A number of measures have already been taken: already in 2010 Belgium adopted a National Adaptation Strategy 17 , which included the development of a National Adaptation Plan 18 .
This plan was adopted by the National Climate Commission in 2017 to strengthen cooperation and develop synergies between different entities.
To this end, adaptation policies already carried out by the different entities have been mapped and new national measures have been identified to strengthen Belgium's resilience to the expected effects of climate change.

4.5 Cross-cutting Attention Points Each entity also addresses a number of cross-cutting focus points relevant to the long-term strategy.
This chapter presents common points of attention for a successful transition.
A socially just transition The transition to be made by Belgium is a challenge for society as a whole.
Both the regions and the federal level stress the importance of a socially just transition in which no one is left behind.
To this end, they seek to implement measures to promote broad citizen participation, social consultation, appropriate training in new occupations and support for low-income groups and other vulnerable target groups most at risk of the impact of this transition.
Efforts will include efforts to combat energy poverty.
A safe and adequate supply of sustainable and affordable energy Both Flemish and Walloon strategies recognise the need for a safe and sufficient supply of sustainable and affordable energy for Belgian citizens and businesses by 2050.
The success of this challenge is crucial for the transition to take place in a social way, while safeguarding the competitiveness of our companies.
To this end, the Flemish and Walloon strategies propose a number of measures which are also addressed in Section 4.3, such as improving efficiency (in all sectors), exploiting the clean renewable energy potential, developing a flexible and decentralised energy system (including energy storage and demand management).
The three regions also recognise that Belgium will continue to be dependent on energy imports (climatically neutral).
In order to achieve regional strategies, there is therefore a need for a safe and adequate supply of sustainable and affordable energy from abroad.
Each entity recognizes the need for technological breakthroughs and innovations to achieve significant emission reductions in sectors that are difficult to decarbonize or even negative in the long term.
In addition, the importance of ‘system innovations' is stressed (new business models, sharing systems, etc.).
The regions also indicate that the success of the transition will depend on supporting the development of innovations to move towards a more circular economy.
Investment and Financing Regional strategies pay particular attention to funding needs.
Indeed, the transition requires significant investments in all sectors concerned and can therefore only be achieved with sufficient funding.
The Brussels and Flemish strategies already include an initial estimate of the expected funding needs (based on existing studies), and all entities will continue their work to identify these needs more accurately.
To meet funding needs, each regional strategy offers specific accents.

THE NECESSITY OF A CLIMATE STRATEGY FLAMES A STRATEGY THAT PURPOSES SUSTAINABLE TRANSITION Greenhouse gas emissions are caused by different processes and applications in all sectors of society.
Therefore, a specific measure in a given sector cannot solve the problem alone: sustained efforts by all sectors are essential to achieve the targeted reductions.
In many cases, the potential for emission reductions within existing systems is limited and we will rely on systemic changes.
Success can only be achieved over the next few decades through a sustained and broad-based transition, where everyone looks in the same direction.
That is why the Flemish Government is committed to an ambitious and realistic climate strategy.
In this way, at Flanders level, we can join forces to work together towards a common final goal, thus enabling us to mobilize more resources, achieve savings and assume our role as an innovative precursor.
On the other hand, a long-term perspective can also help to avoid potential blockages, i.e. investments or habits that hinder further reductions at a later stage.
A long-term strategy must be both flexible and bold.
The future is unpredictable.
The long-term strategy of Flanders will therefore be regularly evaluated in order to adapt to future developments, economic reality and political and social preferences.
On the other hand, 2050 is not so far away, and even close in terms of systemic changes.
So we no longer have time to wait for some technological breakthroughs, but we will focus fully on the elements that, clearly, will be indispensable in a climate-neutral world (the so-called options without regret).
Citizens, businesses and civil society organisations therefore expect a strong authority, which sets clear long-term milestones and sets out a way forward to achieve the desired ambitions.
The role of the Authority is to create a clear framework within which actors can initiate the transition.
This framework will provide the appropriate incentives and ensure that climate-friendly investments and efforts are cost-effective.
Investments that are incompatible with a climate neutral world will not be recommended in due course.
Through this strategy, the Flemish Government intends to meet these expectations.
It postulates a clear ambition by 2050 and examines, by social system, the efforts and progress needed to achieve this objective.
It also examines the important preconditions for making the transition feasible and affordable.

EUROPEAN CLIMATE OBJECTIVES In 2009, EU leaders agreed to reduce European emissions by 80-95% compared to 1990, by 2050, on the basis of the efforts that, according to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, industrialised countries must make to limit warming to 2°C.
In 2011, the EU finally approved the Roadmap to a competitive low carbon economy by 2050 (COM (2011) 112), which includes the main steps and measures needed to achieve this long-term goal at the best cost.
To achieve a minimum reduction of 80% by 2050, European emissions need to be reduced by 25% to 30% by 2020, 40% by 2030 and 60% by 2040.
In line with these steps, the EU committed in 2015 under the Paris Agreement to reduce emissions by at least 40% by 2030 compared to 1990.
In November 2018, the European Commission published a proposal for a long-term strategy for the EU entitled ‘A clean planet for all'.
This proposal highlights the ambition to achieve by 2050 a general balance between emissions and removals of greenhouse gases (= zero net emissions or climate neutrality) at EU level.
The Commission's proposal is accompanied by an extensive in-depth analysis which draws up different scenarios for achieving such zero net emissions.
The Commission's proposal was discussed at the European Council in June 2019 and received the support of a large majority of Member States.
The December 2019 European Council ratified the goal of achieving a climate-neutral EU by 2050, taking into account the latest available scientific data and the need to strengthen global climate action.
Flanders considers it important that this objective of European climate neutrality by 2050 takes into account the following principles:
• each Member State can first examine the extent to which it is able to contribute to these ambitious objectives and communicate its contribution to the European Commission (= bottom-up approach);
• the European Commission can then check to what extent the aggregate ambition of national contributions corresponds to the European objective.
In the case of an ambition gap, the additional efforts required will be allocated in an economically efficient manner among the member States; and 的
In order to achieve these long-term European ambitions, each Member State will have to make drastic and drastic emission reductions.
For most economic sectors, zero emissions will become the norm across national borders.
However, both the IPCC and the European Commission recognize that it is extremely difficult, if not impossible, to avoid certain emissions.
To achieve climate neutrality, they will therefore have to be offset by greenhouse gas removals (or so-called "negative" emissions) in soils, forests and through technological solutions such as CCS and CUC.
EU-wide or even global climate neutrality therefore does not mean that each country or even region will be climate neutral.
Some countries and regions will have residual emissions while others (such as forested regions) will have negative emissions.
The important thing is to achieve a balance between the two, as required by the Paris Agreement.
Flanders is a densely populated region with relatively little space for additional forests and limited potential for geological storage of CO2.
It will therefore be very difficult to achieve climate neutrality within the Flemish territory.
With a reduction of 85%, we are indeed making a significant contribution to Europe's ambition to be climate neutral by 2050.
Technological and social developments can allow us to go further than this 85%.

3 FUTURE VISION FOR THE VARIOUS SECTOR 3.1 ELECTRICITY, FUEL AND HEAT PRODUCTION 3.1.1 Ambition Flanders aims to convert its energy system by 2050 into a climate-neutral, sustainable, reliable and affordable energy system.
In the long term, the share of fossil emissions will systematically decrease in the electricity mix to completely disappear by 2050, so that the system will only have sustainable electricity.
In other sectors, such as transport, building heating and industry, we plan to shift from fossil fuels to green electricity and climate-neutral fuels 7 such as biomass, renewable gas, hydrogen or synthetic fuels.
Although significant benefits can still be achieved in terms of energy efficiency, this increased electrification will lead to an increase in electricity demand compared to today.
We will therefore ensure that by 2050 our electricity system produces enough carbon-free electricity to support other sectors in their decarbonisation through a transition to electrification.
In addition to carbon-free electricity and climate-neutral fuels, heat and cold produced from renewable sources will naturally also have an important role to play in the energy landscape of tomorrow.
In Europe as a whole, heat and cooling account for 50% of final energy consumption.
Projections in 2050 indicate that this energy vector will remain at least as important.
Thermal energy will therefore have to make an important contribution to the achievement of energy and climate objectives.
In addition to electrification of heat and cold (e.g. heat pumps), we exploit as much as possible the present residual heat potential.
Climate-neutral fuels will meet the excess heat demand.
The role of heat and cold produced from renewable sources will also be discussed further in the industrial, construction and agricultural sectors.

Towards a decentralized and flexible electrical system Energy sources such as wind and sun have the disadvantage of not being able to meet our energy needs continuously due to their variable production.
The increase in these sources in the electrical mix therefore creates a major challenge in terms of security of supply.
We are therefore moving, as far as possible, from a centralized, demand-driven energy system to a decentralised, supply-based energy system that offers much more flexibility, in which we are increasingly adapting our consumption to the availability of renewable sources.
In the future, citizens and businesses will provide more of their own energy supply, making the distinction between producers and consumers more blurred.

3.2 INDUSTRY 3.2.1 Ambition Flemish energy-intensive industry is the driving force behind Flanders' economy and is a substantial source of value added and jobs.
Similarly, this industry provides the necessary products and materials that allow reductions in other sectors (e.g., renewable energy fleets, efficient and zero-emission vehicles and housing, etc.) and will continue to play this role in the future.
At the same time, the Flemish industry is still a major source of greenhouse gas emissions and a conversion to more climate-friendly production systems will be needed.
We are therefore committed to supporting our Flemish industry by 2050 in the transition to climate-friendly production systems while maintaining and strengthening its major role in the Flanders economy.
It would not be very useful to achieve the reductions to be achieved through the relocation of production if this results in a net increase in global emissions (e.g., increased emissions for freight transport).
Instead, we are working on an export model so that in Flanders we can not only focus on our limited share of global emissions, but also contribute to the fight against global climate change.
For our non-ETS industry – which is not covered by the ETS emission ceiling – we aim to limit greenhouse gas emissions to 1.2 Mt CO2eq. by 2050.
The achievement of this ambition requires a reversal of the trend which, given the limits of further improvements in terms of efficiency, is only possible by relying on a far-reaching innovation.
In this regard, we abandon the existing industrial model, mainly linear and based on the use of fossil fuels as primary energy sources and raw materials.
By mobilizing resources for the development of new technologies and innovative and climate-neutral production processes, we are contributing to the industrial transition.
In consultation with the sectors concerned, we are developing a strategy for the export of our technological solutions that help meet the climate challenge.
With this leverage, Flanders, a small but prosperous region, can make a positive contribution to the global transition to a climate-neutral society that goes well beyond simply reducing our territorial emissions, while creating new opportunities for its industry.

Within the industrial sector, various technical options and innovations are available to convert to a low-emission industry.
In Flanders, we want to focus on the following axes:
Ongoing commitment to efficiency improvements First of all, industry continues to commit to energy efficiency, energy savings and process improvements.
Through a targeted industrial energy efficiency policy in Flanders, energy-intensive enterprises will receive the necessary incentives to invest in energy saving measures.
In addition to further short-term reductions, this will contribute to security of supply and energy independence while also enhancing competitiveness.
By 2050, industrial residual heat will be recovered to the maximum.
The heat that cannot be used in industry will be used for heating buildings via heat networks.
The circular economy as a basic framework Strong reliance on the circular economy can make a very significant contribution to our climate ambitions.
A study conducted by Material Economics estimates the reduction potential for European industry at 56% by 2050.
Plastic recycling (instead of incineration), mainly, can make a significant contribution.
In addition, these reductions would be more cost-effective and would provide additional benefits in terms of energy, raw materials and water consumption compared to supply-side reduction technologies.
We are therefore committed to a fully circular economy by 2050.
This means that products are manufactured with a minimal input of raw materials, are shared as much as possible (e.g., shared vehicles), their lifespan is extended as much as possible (through sustainable design and repairs) and that, at the end of life, products are recycled to the maximum.
The natural cycle of circular economy generates goods and services from organic matter within a closed circuit.
By increasing the use of sustainable biomass in industry, we are reducing the demand for mineral and fossil raw materials and helping to create new local value chains.
By encouraging their use in applications for a longer life, as in the building sector, we also achieve a large carbon storage.
The longer the supply and disposal chain and the longer the distance between the producer, consumer and repairer, the longer the recycler, the longer the length of the supply and disposal chain, the longer the distance between the producer, consumer and repairer, the longer the length of the supply and disposal chain.
We therefore rely on the proximity of companies that manufacture, maintain, repair and recover sustainable products at the end of their lifespan for reuse and recycling.
In this way, we also ensure that manufacturers have access to a local reservoir of reusable and recyclable raw materials.
By 2050, final treatment of waste by landfill or incineration is almost no longer practised.
Waste collectors and processors have become suppliers of new raw materials that advise their customers to use the material flows as efficiently as possible.
Only non-transformable residues resulting from these recycling processes are still eligible for incineration or landfill.
Incineration is therefore only carried out in installations ensuring a quality recovery of the released energy.
Conversion to renewable and climate-neutral fuels and raw materials At present, the industry is a major consumer of fossil fuels: oil, coal and natural gas.
In the future, part of this consumption will no longer be electrified.
In addition, the industry will in part switch to climate-neutral fuels.
To the extent that this is economically justified, further electrification of industry can also lead to drastic emission reductions.
We can use sustainable biomass as a source of carbon molecules in the chemistry sector.
In doing so, we use available biomass for high value-added production processes.
Biomass should be valued in relation to its combustion for energy supply.
Electrolysis can transform water into sustainable hydrogen with electricity.
This molecule can be used as a raw material in chemistry, for example in combination with nitrogen or CO2 for the production of raw materials such as ammonia and methanol.
Given the limited local supply of bio-based, renewable and climate-neutral fuels and raw materials in Flanders, enhanced collaboration between agriculture and industry will be needed.
A part of the product will also have to be imported.
CO2 capture and reuse Energy efficiency measures, the exploitation of climate neutral sources and material reuse reduce dependence on fossil fuels and lead to CO2 reductions.
However, if these measures result in insufficient emission reductions, CO2 capture and storage or reuse (carbon capture, use and storage or CCUS) is a promising path for the elimination of the remaining emissions.
In the long term, CO2 emissions will remain as much as possible in a closed circuit via the CUC process.
In order to facilitate CCUS applications, the Government is examining the creation of a ‘dorsal' of CO2: a pipeline network dedicated to (temporary) storage, transport and use of CO2 to promote the circular carbon economy.
Industrial Symbiosis in Clusters Flemish industry is strongly concentrated in clusters around certain logistic nodes, such as the Port d'Anvers and the North Sea Port.
This allows the facilities present to exchange and value residual flows – such as CO2, H2 and residual heat.
We take advantage of this asset and rely, through a clustering logic, on an industrial symbiosis consisting of the maximum exchange and valorisation of residual flows.
Companies will need to engage in intensive cooperation and exchange of information in order to allow for their production processes.

Smart and enjoyable urban cities and hubs Within the different transport regions, important social functions must be accessible to everyone in an easy and safe way by (collective) sustainable means of transport or a combination of them.

4.1 MAJOR EFFECTS OF CLIMATE CHANGE IN FLANDER Greenhouse gas emissions are leading to climate change.
Since the beginning of the 19th century surveys, the average temperature in our country has increased by almost 2.5°C.
We now know at least one heat wave every summer, whereas in the 1970s this phenomenon occurred only one in three years.
Evaporation increased faster than annual precipitation, resulting in a decrease in water availability.
And the average sea level at the coast is now 13 cm higher than in the early 1950s. 的 的 的 的 的 的 的 的 的 ///////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////// page 35 of 47 Flemish Climate Strategy
On the basis of the most recent data, it is not excluded that the average annual temperature in Flanders continues to rise with, on average, more days of heat wave.
Total annual precipitation is estimated to increase, with wetter winters and drier summers.
Not only the averages, but also the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events can change.
A decrease in precipitation in summer and an increase in evaporation during the summer months increase the risk of extreme drought (as in 1976 and 2018) once every 50 years in the current climate to once every four to five years in 2100.
This can lead to reduced flow rates, degradation of surface water quality, damage to agriculture and endanger the supply of drinking water.
The extent to which the climate change described above will indeed continue and its speed depend closely on the success of a global policy to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and the transition to a climate neutral society.
But to compensate for or at least mitigate the potential effects and impact on man, nature and the economy, in addition to a policy of mitigation, an evolutionary adaptation policy will also be required.

Looking ahead The 2050 vision for the energy sector is based on total decarbonisation , with the energy mix entirely based on renewable energy.
Any remaining thermal power plants should be associated with carbon capture.
This climate objective combines the need to continue to provide citizens and businesses with safe, sustainable and affordable energy.
All sectors are affected by this transition and contribute to it according to their specificities.
The total decarbonisation of the energy system is based on three pillars: the decentralization of electricity production, digitisation and electrification of part of the consumption of the final sectors where appropriate and where no more efficient solution exists.
These three trends provide solutions but also represent major challenges.
They will have to allow for the integration of large and variable shares of renewable energy into the grid while ensuring security of supply for citizens and energy companies at affordable/competitive prices.
In particular, this will require the participation and empowerment of the consumer actor.
It will be important to promote synergies between renewable local production and consumption in order to strengthen the system in parallel with the introduction of flexibility measures 12 .
The integration of the Belgian electricity system into the European electricity system will be strengthened.
The electricity sector has a strong European dimension and it is important to maximise the strengths and synergies of Member States in order to pool efforts, minimize redundancy and ensure a minimum societal cost.
A coherent strategy must combine regional implementation with vision and implementation at European level 13 .

A decentralised system The establishment of a decentralised system of energy production is a key element of the energy transition involving the consumer.
The latter will have to participate in the implementation of energy demand flexibility to facilitate the integration of new energy sources.
The promotion and establishment of citizen or renewable energy communities is an opportunity to allow for direct involvement of the citizen and thus a greater acceptance of the future transition.
The same applies to the mechanisms of collective self-consumption which will allow access to energy production from renewable sources for inhabitants of apartment buildings, etc.
A flexible energy system The current flexibility needs are mainly supported by flexible thermal power plants.
Their aim is to smooth the electricity demand curve by reducing both seasonal and daily peaks in consumption.
By 2050, it is proposed that this type of power station should be dispensed with, which will require the development of alternative flexibility solutions.
Measures will be taken to ensure the continued development of new centralized and decentralized storage tools and of industrial and personal load shifting services in order to ensure a better match between supply and demand.
In addition, the deployment of smart meters will provide the technical opportunity for those who wish to be active in the market, particularly in the provision of their flexibility.
These same meters will also provide a better knowledge of consumption and will be the gateway for new services aimed both at reducing consumption and at moving it.
The development of smart networks is coupled with "smartization" and data management.
There is no need to set targets for them, but to determine the main rules underlying their development:
• Maximising the capacity of infrastructure (for production tools and flexible tools) and synchronization by optimising the collective well-being of the electricity system as a whole;
• Remuneration of networks according to performance indicators);
A system that relies on digitisation Digitisation provides technical solutions to manage the integration of increasing shares of renewable energy into the network and to include companies and citizens producing in the market.
However, it represents a considerable cost, particularly because of the infrastructure needed to enable real-time data management.
In addition, the growth in data management has already led to cybersecurity issues.
Finally, the energy demand of these data management centres must also be taken into account.
Rational electrification through sectoral coupling and power-to-X Thanks to the deployment of competitive renewable electricity, the energy system in 2050 will be marked by electrification in many sectors such as heat, transport and industry, whether by direct use of electricity, hydrogen, or by the production of efuels.
This electrification will help to develop the surplus variable renewable electricity.
This electrification will be based on a sectoral coupling between the energy sector and the other sectors of construction, transport and industry and a vector-based coupling between electricity, gas and heat/climateisation.
Indeed, different sectors and vectors need to be interconnected in order to allow the injection of an increasing share of renewable electricity into the grid and its optimal recovery.
This leads to the need to link electricity generation, network management, the introduction of storage solutions of different types in the building (see Chapter 3.2 Building), or through coupling 14 with the gas network and through sectoral integration with transport and industry.
Finally, Power-to-X will contribute to the balance and security of the energy system, particularly through the production of green hydrogen (see Table 2).

Storage solutions Storage solutions that are necessary for different time horizons will have to be deployed in a balanced manner.

Major car manufacturers in partnership with electricians, begin the test phases of this new technology.
The citizen could also find a win-win solution.
It will be possible to charge the vehicle at the lowest price of electricity (by definition at off-peak hours) to return it when the price is higher.

Vision for the future The study of possible scenarios for a low-carbon transition in the Region indicates that the construction sector has a very high potential contribution to enable the Region to reduce its emissions.
The "Bas Carbon Wallonia" study envisages a 90% to 100% reduction in emissions from the building sector (scenario -80 %).
However, "As the building stock is very slow to renew, the new stringent standards for heavy construction or renovation will not be sufficient to improve the sector's performance in a tangible way by 2050.
In order to improve the energy performance of the sector, large-scale actions are therefore also needed across the existing fleet, since the vast majority of it will still be present in 50 years' time."15
Most building components have a long lifespan.
The components that have the greatest impact on energy consumption will only be renovated once by 2050.
Investments must therefore be made in the right sequence to ensure that the various interventions realize their potential.
Similarly, the investments that will be made in the future will have to reach directly the highest energy standards to be achieved by 2050.
They will have to be ‘future proof'.
In view of these elements, the renovation of the Walloon Buildings Park is a key issue in achieving energy and climate objectives.
Indeed, it enables energy efficiency to be improved by reducing consumption.
It contributes to the achievement of Objectives 15 Meyer S. and Marshal K., 2016.

This is why Wallonia has a long-term renovation strategy (2050), after consultation with stakeholders, taking into account the characteristics of the existing building stock and the specific features of the Region, which aims to:

Ø For the tertiary sector: moving in 2050 towards a tertiary energy-neutral (zero energy) building fleet for heating, sanitary hot water, cooling and lighting.
The priorities proposed in the strategy correspond to the most cost-effective measures, i.e. the deep renovation of the less efficient dwellings (the insulation of the envelope, with priority on roofs, without neglecting the replacement of end-of-life systems).
However, this renovation must be carried out by ensuring that any renovation project is integrated into a comprehensive reflection consistent with the objectives of the Region and that it does not lead to unavailability of housing.
A phase of the renovation rate to be achieved in order to achieve the 2050 target is proposed in the strategy and declined in periods of 5 years.
This phase allows us to check whether Wallonia is on the right track to achieve its objectives.
Not all the buildings covered by the strategy will be able to meet the average park-wide target and other more comprehensive tracks will have to be examined in the future. In addition, the expansion of the new, efficient building fleet is one of the elements that will contribute to the park's improvement and overall objective.
In this regard, it is important to highlight the development of new urban renewal policies, which aim, inter alia, to combat global warming and to "optimize local production/resources and energy consumption".
New construction, with very high performance requirements, will also have to contribute to the overall objectives.
It is expected that there will be around 15,000 new housing units each year, adding to the current stock of about 1.5 million housing units.
Estimates of the effect of these measures should also take into account emissions from building construction.
The phases of renovation take into account the need to allocate investments and to plan investments with the greatest potential impact as a priority.
Although the phases of renovation measures are determined on a case-by-case basis (in a building-specific renovation road map), it is expected that, overall for the entire park, the major step-by-step energy renovation will focus on roof renovation, the remainder of the project will then be completed and the systems will be renovated and renewable energy sources installed.
Beyond the fact that it will allow the reduction of GHG emissions from buildings and in the long term the increased use of renewable energy sources, the deep energy renovation of the building stock will generate significant co-benefits such as creation and relocation of employment, reduction of energy insecurity and energy dependence, increased comfort and indoor air quality, ... while aiming at a social issue of internal restructuring of the park to meet future needs.

Moving towards a carbon-neutral energy mix in buildings A set of buildings, and certainly existing buildings, will continue to consume energy for the production of heat, sanitary hot water and also, increasingly, for air conditioning.
The decarbonization of heat production (heating and sanitary hot water) will be carried out in the vast majority of buildings through electrification (mainly heat pumps), the use of biomass, thermal solar, cogeneration and where necessary by pooling needs through heat networks, particularly in cities.
The use of fossil fuels will be discouraged and phased out.
Passive solutions will be highlighted for cooling (night cooling, free cooling...) and specific attention to summer comfort will be developed and implemented (solar protections, inertial enhancement, site etc.) in order to limit the use of active air conditioning.
In exceptional cases (classified buildings, ...), if a fossil energy source is to be used, it will be used through highly efficient technologies.
A GHG emission indicator will be developed as part of the regulatory framework and included in the BEP certificate.
Finally, as part of the sector coupling and Power-to-X, buildings will become an integral part of the energy system by providing electricity storage potential to absorb or inject excess or shortfalls in renewable energy production or even to provide ancillary services to the grids.

Transport (Mobility and freight) 3.4.1.
State of play Transport is the only area where both CO2 emissions and energy consumption have been growing since 2008.
This trend is linked to the increase in road traffic in Wallonia (+57% since 1990) and a relative stagnation or even decrease in the use of other modes of public transport (whether in terms of passenger or freight transport).
Transport accounts for about 25% of Walloon energy consumption and CO2 emissions.
If we remove the sectors covered by the ETS, transport becomes the largest contributor.
Road transport alone uses almost 90% of the energy used in transport, whether it is passenger or freight transport, which accounts for 60% and 40% of the total.
However, the importance of transit traffic in Wallonia for goods, on which the Region has very little catch, should be stressed.
Air freight accounts for a higher share than air passenger transport.
A number of notable factors are noted in the vehicle fleet: - Despite a certain reduction in mileage per vehicle over time, the fleet continued to grow at a rate that was substantially the same as population growth.
The only significant event in recent years is the slow de-dieselisation of the fleet due to a significant increase in the petrol stock and a very modest increase in alternative fuels - a fairly large increase in light commercial vehicles, which now constitute 86% of the utility fleet following the introduction of the kilometric levy for vehicles over 3.5 T 3.4.2.
Vision 2050 A fully decarbonized sector By 2050, transport will be completely decarbonized.
This decarbonisation will be based on a reduction in mobility needs resulting from a change in land use.
This improved demand management will effectively reduce greenhouse gas emissions, but also improve air quality and hence improve health.
In addition, it will allow a pacified management of the common public space.
However, only scenarios involving a significant modal shift to public transport and active travel modes will allow cities to be de-bogganed.
Similarly for the transport of goods, only a modal shift from the truck to the rail or the waterway will result in a reduction in congestion.
A growing share of shared and autonomous transport is clearly possible in this time horizon.
Fuels of any kind (electricity, gas, hydrogen, biofuels and synthetic fuels) will be of renewable origin.
The objective of neutrality by 2050 requires almost total decarbonisation of the transport sector, either through the switch to electric energy or through sustainable biofuels and biogas.
However, a share of non-biofuels is reserved for air and sea transport in 2050, with a focus on recycled fuels or synthetic fuels from CO2 (e-fuels).

Agriculture and forestry 3.5.1.
State of play According to the latest available inventory, the Walloon forest stores approximately 900 kt of CO2 annually in forest biomass, representing 2.5% of Walloon anthropogenic emissions.
The annual afforestation/reforestation balance, which represents a net emission of about 300 kt, must be deducted from this storage.
Forested and deforested areas are practically in equilibrium annually, but this leads to net emissions because deforestation emissions are recorded in the year, while reforestation only leads to progressive storage of CO2 by ecosystems.
Finally, carbon stored over the longer term in harvested wood products (wood, panels, paper and paperboard) is accounted for and is also a relatively limited well 23 .
Agriculture emits CH4 and N2O, as well as CO2.
In 2017, the agricultural sector emitted just under 5 million tonnes of 2-equivalent CO (4862 kt CO 2-eq), or about 14% of Wallonia's GHG emissions.
These emissions are characterized by significant quantities of methane (CH 4) and nitrous oxide (N 2O) and to a lesser extent CO 2.
Methane was produced by 92% of the enteric fermentation of ruminants and 8% of the effluent management.
Nitrogen oxide (N 2O) is produced at 93% of soil and 7% of effluent management.
Finally, CO2 comes from the lime, urea and fuel of tractors and other agricultural machinery.
At the soil carbon level, which is accounted for separately, cropland is a source of emissions, estimated at 513 kt CO2-eq in 2017, while grasslands are a carbon sink, estimated at 885 kt CO 2-eq in 2017.
The main source of emissions is land-use change, in particular the conversion of grasslands to cropland.
Part of the CO2 stored in the grasslands is mineralized when the land is turned.
The overall agricultural land balance for CO2 is thus a storage of approximately 370 kt CO 2-eq per year, or about 8% of the other GHG emissions in the sector presented above.
Available projections suggest that CH4 and N2O emissions from the agricultural sector will continue to decline by 2030.
At the forest level, particularly given the age-class structure and species changes, such as Douglas-fir planting after spruce cutting, the forest well is expected to increase slightly by 2050 24 .
This does not take into account the potential impact of natural disturbances, such as droughts, fires, storms or pests, aggravated by climate change.
Vision 2050 Biomass and agricultural and forestry soils can be significant carbon sinks, but under current accounting rules, both at European and international level, only part of the storage is accounted for against the reduction targets, with a view to accounting for only emissions and removals directly related to human intervention, such as forest management change or land-use change.
Under these complex rules, forest biomass and Walloon soils could be accounted for as a very limited well or even a net source of emissions during the period 2021-2030.
In the context of the 2050 strategy and compliance with the Paris Agreements, a European reflection is therefore essential on the future accounting arrangements for the LULUCF sector, which will largely condition the possibility of a balance between emissions and carbon sinks.

Towards a just and equitable transition The concept of "just transition" aims at the organization and transformation of our society into a carbon-poor economy that is socially just and that carries a sustainable society project, based on the values of impartiality and equity and does not strengthen the dualization of society (social, economic, technological, etc.).
The aim here is to seize the energy transition to invent a type of development that is both respectful of the environment and natural resources, but also socially fair and equitable for workers and citizens.
More broadly, the ability to react to a climate event; access to adaptation or some less polluting technologies is influenced by social inequalities already existing within a country.
Therefore, combating climate change , as well as adapting to climate change , also implies a form of reflection on social justice.
This will need to be part of a dialogue with stakeholders and citizens.
The global trade union movement defines "just transition" as "a comprehensive strategy to protect people whose jobs, income and means of subsistence are compromised by climate policies", but it also "is intended to support workers and communities affected by the negative effects of climate change, and to promote work and training to ensure decent and good quality jobs."37 The two dimensions will therefore be taken into account in the deployment of the strategy.

Identification of the problem According to the Ellen Mac Arthur 46 Foundation, without fossil fuels for our energy system will only allow us to remove 55% of our greenhouse gas emissions.

Our economy is based on the quantities produced and consumed; profits and growth are measured by "gross domestic product".
Our economic growth is based on the consumption of natural resources whose intrinsic cost is not taken into account; resource depletion is not integrated into our economy.
Our natural resource capital is declining and environmental debt is being carried over to future generations.

Adaptation to climate change: a resilient Wallonia Climate change is now a certainty at the global level and is becoming increasingly visible.
Successive IPCC reports warn and recall the human responsibilities associated with this phenomenon.
All parts of the world are likely to be affected.
There is no area or sector of activity that will not feel the effects of it, and therefore the need for necessary adaptation.
The Walloon Region's study on adaptation to climate change 49 provided a comprehensive assessment – characterization, current vulnerabilities, future vulnerabilities – of the Walloon region following seven themes: agriculture, water, infrastructure/land use, health, energy, biodiversity and forest.
An expanded consultation of experts identified the first steps to be taken to adapt the Walloon region to climate change.
Climate projections The climate projections built during the study allow us to trace the major trends in Walloon climate, up to 2050 and even to the end of the century.
These trends are then used to assess the impacts of climate change on the territory.
A warmer climate There is a clear trend of generalised rise in average temperatures:
Between +1.3°C and 2.8°C in 2050 and +2 and +4°C in 2100 compared to the reference period (1961-1990).
Maximum temperatures increase faster than minimum temperatures.
Increased seasonality of precipitation is expected to increase winter precipitation (up to 20% in 2100 compared to 1961-1990) and regional differences are expected to increase with higher precipitation in the Condroz Famenne and Ardennes regions.
Conversely, summers will be drier (with a drop of up to -25% in 2100 compared to 1961-1990) and warmer also with peaks of up to +6°C in the month of August.
In spring and autumn, the increase in temperature is quite marked but the evolution of precipitation is more uncertain.
Towards more episodes of intense rain in winter and more frequent summer heat waves In addition to tendential changes, there are also so-called extreme phenomena.
Thus, there is a trend towards an increase in the number of days per year of very high rainfall (up to +40% of the increase by 2100).
The increase projected is larger and constant for winter, and to some extent for autumn.
From 2050 onwards, the projections agreed on an increase in the number of days of summer heat waves.
On this horizon, there could be up to 18 additional days of heat and the increase would even go up to +28 days in 2100.
Risks Flood risk The Walloon territory is particularly sensitive to the risk of flooding, which can cause considerable damage to buildings and infrastructure: the 262 municipalities in the Walloon Region all experienced at least one flooding event in their territory between 1993 and 2016, whether it was by overflowing streams or runoff.
The vulnerability of Wallonia to this risk 49 ECORES-TEC 2011-Wallonne Region-Study Adaptation to Climate Change
Flood risk is expected to increase with climate change due to the high probability of increasing rainfall and the intensity of winter rains.
Particular attention should therefore be paid to this risk in view of the very large damage and costs which it is likely to generate.
Adaptation deserves to be considered here in the long term, in particular in terms of urban planning (limitation of sprawl, reduction of soil waterproofing, ...).
The risk of soil erosion Soil losses have increased in recent decades in the Walloon region.
This is mainly due to the increase in rainfall erosivity, which is linked to the increase in the average annual amount of precipitation.
Reductions in yields were also observed due to heavy rains and droughts.
Projected increases in the frequency and intensity of winter precipitation will increase the phenomenon of water erosion and thus the vulnerability of the sector in particular agriculture.
Similarly, the expected increase in drought duration exacerbates soil erosion by reducing vegetation cover.
Root crops and bare soils will be particularly vulnerable.
In view of the potential severity of this risk (eroded soil is often a soil lost forever), adaptation, particularly in the agricultural sector, will have to be anticipated because it will not necessarily be spontaneous.
More Risks of Stretch and Increased Water Pollution Models project a gradual decrease in precipitation volumes in summer, combined with a rise in temperatures at the same time.
This may result in a decrease in the amount of water in streams and therefore a higher risk of being reached.
The Meuse basin is particularly vulnerable to this development.
Analysis of historical series on this basin (AMICE project) shows that the months of August, September and October were most subjected to water stress during the past century.
Projections indicate a very significant decrease in Meuse flows between July and October (less than 50m3/s averaged in August, September and October, compared to over 100 m3/s currently) by the end of the century.
In addition, external pressures on the water resource are caused by high heat (e.g. energy sector needs).
The increase in these risks could eventually lead to disruptions or even interruptions in navigation, as has been observed in other countries (Rhin, Loire and Danube during the 2003 droughts).
Water quality will also be affected: pollution will be concentrated in smaller volumes of water masses.
Risks related to summer heat - The health risk related to summer heat and the increase in urban heat content The increase in minimum and maximum temperatures and the rise in the number of days of summer heat waves will have the direct effect of increasing the risk of summer mortality, especially among people at risk such as the elderly (cardiovascular problems and respiratory problems) and especially in cities.
In addition, the number of people at risk may increase as a result of the population's projected ageing, which would indirectly create additional vulnerability.

Risks to the energy system and the links with the mitigation process 51 Changes in the short-term electricity production system (from a centralized system heavily based on nuclear and fossil fuels to a decentralized system dominated by renewable energy) driven mainly by climate change will require a very large scale adaptation both in terms of infrastructure (dimensioning and management of transport networks, production capacity, etc.) and changes in consumption patterns, in particular electricity.
These changes will have to take into account the climate impacts at work and not just the mitigation problem.
In addition to major network changes, Wallonia will most likely face an increase in energy demand (including electricity) for cooling and air conditioning during the warm months.
Misadaptation to this impact may increase the vulnerability of the sector .
By 2050, it is imperative to examine the risks posed by climate change impacts to the energy system as it may be in that future.
Conversely, it is also relevant to take these impacts into account in making the choices of technologies that we will implement in the transition of the energy system, particularly with regard to the use of different forms of renewable energy, in particular biomass.
The risks to the energy system are of several kinds, they will have to be managed in a comprehensive manner in order to avoid misadjustment and to allow a full vision of the synergies and the necessary trade-offs with the mitigation policy.
Firstly, rising temperatures could lead to a reduction in energy requirements for heat production, but it could mainly lead to an increase in consumption due to air conditioning resulting in peak consumption.
Water availability is an uncontourned element for energy production, while energy is also needed for the production of drinking water.
Drought periods may lead to water availability problems for cooling thermal power plants.
Cooling penuries for thermal power plants and nuclear reactors in Wallonia arise, for the period up to 2025, due to the closure of the last Tihange reactor.
Some technologies that are being promoted to combat global warming, such as the CCS, can pose problems as it reduces the efficiency of thermal power plants and increases their water needs.
Reduced water availability may therefore limit the feasibility of the CCS.
Security of supply can be affected during heat waves, especially if it coincides with periods of drought and therefore reduced availability of water for cooling thermal power plants.
Heat waves can also impact electricity transport infrastructure by reducing the transmission capacity of power lines.

Adapting an energy system in transition In general, a resilient energy system will be based on a mix of renewable energy sources combined with sufficient interconnections and a variety of flexibility solutions.
The risks of climate change impacts on the energy system can be managed by different types of technical and ad hoc actions or by setting up mechanisms and studies of structural risk impacts.
On the side of technical and specific adaptation measures, with a view to enhancing security of supply during heatwaves, it is possible to identify the installation of drivers with operating limits at higher temperatures in the transport lines, the imposition of strict standards of efficiency for air conditioning equipment which will reduce peak consumption, increase transport capacity including for international connections, sufficient back-up capacity and transfer to electricity generation technologies.
The demand for air conditioning can also be reduced by improving the design of buildings in terms of insulation, orientation, the installation of solar protections for windows and by using non-electric air conditioning technologies, such as absorption systems that enhance heat.
In addition, there are synergies and trade-offs between mitigation measures and adaptation measures. !!!!
